Which clock does an autonomous adversary actually move?

A Monte Carlo model of exploit availability against remediation, rerun on every change. The adversary timeline is measured, not assumed: days from CVE publication to public exploit code. That clock has sat at its floor: the median has not exceeded one day in any complete year since 2015. Three other mechanisms have not: the share of bugs weaponised, post-exploitation tempo, and the rate new vulnerabilities are found.

01

Exploit availability against remediation

Day zero is CVE publication. Everything to the left of it is exploit code that precedes the record — mostly publication lag, not zero-days. The hatched region is remediation that completes with exploit code already public; the badge is that probability across all remediation, drawn window or not.

remediation complete exploit available public before the CVE record unremediated exposure

How to read this chart

The horizontal axis is days from CVE publication. Day 0 is the vertical rule, and the shaded band to its left is time when no CVE record existed yet. Most of that band is not a zero-day. It is publication lag: exploit code is already public and mass scanning has already followed it, so the model treats that time as fully exposed rather than discounting it the way it discounts a genuine pre-disclosure exploit. The same series puts 98.5% of exploits ahead of publication in 2000, at a median of 44 days. That measures how slowly records were published, not what attackers could do.

Two distributions mirror off the centre line. Above it, remediation complete is when your remediation lands, day by day. Below it, exploit available is when public exploit code appears, drawn twice: as a faint daily density, and as the bold cumulative curve. The cumulative curve reads against its own scale, which runs from 0% at the centre line down to 100% at the bottom.

Unremediated exposure is the hatched region. For each day it is the part of that day's remediation for which exploit code already existed, so it is the share of the remediation curve that lost the race.

The two large figures answer different questions. The marker on the cumulative curve at day 0 is the share of exploits that are already public when the CVE record lands. The badge is the share that arrive before your remediation completes, which is always the later test of the two and therefore always the larger number. The percentages at the edges are exploit or remediation mass falling outside the drawn window, labelled there rather than folded into the end bins, which would invent a spike that is not in the data.

02

Attrition from publication to compromise

Each stage is a subset of the one above it. The stream entering the top is every severity band, not just Critical: you set the Critical count, and the High, Medium and Low volumes beside it are derived from published ratios, each with its own rate of acquiring exploit code and of being exploited. Counting Criticals alone would discard two thirds of everything known to be exploited. The two largest reductions happen outside the remediation process: most published vulnerabilities land on a version or configuration that is not exposed to them, and most of the rest never get a working exploit.

03

Sensitivity analysis

Each bar varies a single parameter across its plausible range, holding everything else fixed. Read the ranking rather than the ratio between bars: bar length depends on the range chosen for each parameter. The baseline marked on the chart is recomputed at this chart's own trial count with the uncertain inputs held central, so it can sit a point or two from the headline.

A compromise is an adversary establishing a foothold. It becomes an incident when detection and response fail to contain it before the adversary reaches its objective. The two respond to different parameters, and that difference is what should drive priority.

Two bars need a caveat. Detection is flat against compromise because detection only enters the model once a foothold exists, so controls that block exploitation outright sit outside the model rather than measuring as zero. Headcount is a measure of size rather than a control, and the routes that scale with people scale less than proportionally, because one lure reaches every mailbox at once.

    BOD 26-04 names adversary use of AI as a reason the window between patch release and exploitation may narrow. That is four separate claims with different evidence behind them and different consequences, so each gets its own dial. All four default to the measured record.

    is flat against compromise because speed from foothold to objective cannot change whether you were breached, only whether anyone reached it in time. Switch the readout to incidents and it lifts off the floor. Even there the curve understates where it bites: against a 24/7 SOC, full tempo removes roughly half of the margin that detection buys over having none. A faster adversary does not beat detection on any single intrusion. It devalues the investment.

    The steepest curve here is the last one, and it is not a clock at all. asks how many vulnerabilities are found in software you already run: the size of the stream rather than the speed of anything downstream of it. Machine-found bugs in real codebases are a capability that exists today rather than a forecast, which makes this the least speculative of the four and, on most estates, the largest.

    08

    Why severity is not the prioritisation trigger

    The conventional approach counts Critical-rated CVEs. The published record does not support it. Below is the full scored CVE population, bucketed by CVSS band, against the share of each band that has reached the confirmed-exploited catalogue.

    CVSS bandScored CVEsConfirmed exploitedRate

    This position is now codified. CISA Binding Operational Directive 26-04 (10 June 2026) supersedes BOD 19-02 and BOD 22-01 and removes severity as the trigger entirely. It prioritises on four SSVC decision points: presence in the KEV catalogue, public exposure of the asset, whether exploitation is automatable, and the technical impact obtained. Remediation tiers run from three days plus forensic triage down to “fix on system upgrade”. The published heuristic for automatable is a public proof-of-concept achieving remote code execution that runs reliably, which is the same test this model applies.

    09

    Severity-label inflation

    The Critical-rated population is growing faster than the CVE population that contains it. A model anchored to criticals per year therefore drifts more pessimistic each year for reasons unrelated to the estate being assessed. This is the second reason severity is not used as an input here.

    Part of that increase reflects scoring policy rather than vulnerability reality. CNAs self-assign scores, and the share each rates 9.0 or above ranges from near zero to roughly two in five against the same scale. NVD has also stopped analysing a growing share of published CVEs.

    10

    Model provenance

    Every input carries one of three labels, kept separate rather than blended. measured is read directly from the vulnerability data shipped with this page and can be reproduced offline. reported is a dated published figure: cited, but dependent on someone else's method. assumed is judgement, used where no public measurement exists. The last two are redrawn from their stated range throughout every run, which is where the uncertainty bands come from. If you disagree with a number, the range beside it is the thing to argue with.

    Method

    60,000 simulated years for every change you make, with the uncertain inputs redrawn 653 times across that run, dropping to 4,000 years while a control is being dragged. The adversary timeline is sampled from the measured distribution of days between CVE publication and public exploit code. The remediation timeline is built from triage delay, emergency-change capability and routine cadence. A compromise arrives as a campaign that can reach several systems at once, rather than as an independent roll per system, which would saturate near 100% and overstate the result.

    Provenance

    Vulnerability, exploit-catalogue, EPSS and KEV figures are from the CyberMon snapshot …. That snapshot ships with this page rather than being fetched when it loads, so every measured figure can be reproduced offline. The data, the model and the checks that verify it are in the public repository.

    Scope

    The classes counted and excluded are listed beside the headline figure. Credential abuse has led the DBIR initial-access ranking throughout the series, ahead of vulnerability exploitation. A model built on the patch clock alone therefore misses the larger half of the routes, which is why the classes needing no vulnerability are simulated here rather than folded into a single leftover rate.

    Every input behind those classes is judgement rather than measurement: no public dataset answers them the way the CVE record answers the exploit clock. What can be checked is the mix: at the baseline estate it is calibrated to the reported initial-access distribution, and an automated check fails if it drifts. … Run-rate figures are linear extrapolation from a partial year.

    Sources for the reported tier

    Breakout timing from the CrowdStrike Global Threat Report 2026 (29-minute average eCrime breakout, fastest observed 27 seconds); dwell and notification-path timings from Mandiant M-Trends 2026 (14-day global median, 10 days when an organisation finds it itself against 26 when somebody else tells them); remediation velocity against the Verizon DBIR 2026 edge-device series (a median around a month to full remediation, with roughly half never fully remediated) and the Edgescan and Cyentia vulnerability-management series; containment bounded by Sophos State of Ransomware 2026 (44% of attacks stopped before encryption, which is a per-attack rate, so it is applied to each intrusion rather than to a year as a whole).

    These are vendor incident-response and survey populations, which skew toward organisations that needed help, and none of them can be reproduced from the data shipped with this page the way the CVE figures can. They are cited and dated so you can go and look. The inputs drawn from them carry wide declared ranges, and none of them changes the ranking of the four dials. Everything in the measured tier is regenerated from the snapshot and rechecked automatically on every change.
