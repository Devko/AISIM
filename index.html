The race

Day zero is the day a patch becomes available. Everything left of it is exploitation that began before you had anything to install. The hatched area is not decoration: it is the probability that a working exploit exists before you have closed the hole.

    The compression what-if

    The exploit clock in this model defaults to what was measured, not to a forecast. This curve is the hypothesis: what happens if exploits arrive faster, more of them get written, and more land before disclosure. Note that the measured record does not currently show that compression — see the anchors below.

    Why this model ignores severity

    The obvious way to build this would be to count Critical CVEs. The data says not to. Below is every scored CVE, bucketed by CVSS band, against how many of them ever reached the confirmed-exploited catalogue.

    CVSS bandScored CVEsConfirmed exploitedRate

    This is no longer a contrarian position. CISA's Binding Operational Directive 26-04 (10 June 2026) supersedes BOD 22-01 and drops severity as the trigger entirely. It prioritises on four decision points instead — is it in the KEV catalogue, is the asset publicly exposed, is exploitation automatable, and what technical impact does it give — with the shortest tier at three days plus forensic triage, and the lowest at “fix on system upgrade”. Its published heuristic for automatable is a public proof-of-concept that achieves remote code execution and reliably runs. That is the same primitive this model measures, arrived at independently.

    And the label is inflating

    The count of Critical-rated CVEs is growing faster than the corpus that contains it. A model anchored to "criticals per year" silently gets more pessimistic every year for reasons that have nothing to do with your estate — which is the second reason this one is not.

    Part of that rise is scoring policy rather than vulnerability reality: CNAs assign their own scores, and the share they rate 9.0+ varies from near zero to roughly two in five for the same scale. Meanwhile NVD has stopped analysing a growing share of the corpus outright.

    What the model is anchored on

    Three kinds of number, and the page will not pretend they are the same. measured is read directly off the vendored corpus and is reproducible offline. reported is a dated published figure — cited, but you are trusting somebody else's methodology. assumed is judgement, because no public measurement exists. The last two are drawn from their stated range on every block of trials, which is where the credible bands come from. If you disagree with one, the range is the argument to have.

    Method. One race, simulated 14,000 times per parameter change. The attacker clock is sampled from the measured distribution of days between CVE publication and public exploit code; the defender clock is built from your awareness delay, emergency path and routine cadence. Compromise is drawn as a correlated campaign arrival rather than as independent per-system coin flips, because independent draws would pin the answer at 100% and lie to you.

    Provenance. Vulnerability, exploit-catalogue, EPSS and KEV figures are from the CyberMon snapshot —, vendored under data/cybermon/ so every number here is reproducible offline. Regenerate with node tools/refresh-data.js && node tools/derive-calibration.js. Verify the model with node test/model.test.js.

    Scope. This models vulnerability exploitation only. Credential abuse, phishing and insider routes are absent, so treat the output as a lower bound on intrusion risk rather than a picture of it — the 2026 DBIR puts vulnerability exploitation at 31% of breaches, ahead of credentials but far from all of it. Run-rate figures are linear extrapolation of a partial year.

    Sources for the reported tier. Breakout timing from the CrowdStrike Global Threat Report 2026 (29-minute average eCrime breakout, fastest observed 27 seconds); dwell and notification-path timings from Mandiant M-Trends 2026 (14-day global median, 10 days when an organisation finds it itself against 26 when somebody else tells them); containment corroborated against Sophos State of Ransomware 2026 (44% of attacks stopped before encryption). These are vendor incident-response populations, which skew toward organisations that needed incident response — read them as the shape of the distribution rather than as a population baseline.
